How to switch between Av / Manual modes

Av (camera picks the shutter): flick the AE mode selector, the small switch around the AT dial window, to Av, and turn the lens aperture ring to its green A. Now the AT dial picks f-numbers and the camera picks the shutter speed.

Manual (you pick both): set the AE mode selector to Tv, dial your shutter speed on the AT dial, then turn the aperture ring off A to the f-number you want. An M lights in the viewfinder to confirm you're in manual. The meter still shows what it would have chosen, so you can match it or ignore it.

Learn more

How to read the numbers: plain numbers are fractions of a second — 1000 means the shutter is open for 1/1000th of a second, 60 means 1/60th. A ″ mark means whole seconds: 4″ keeps the shutter open for four full seconds. On the camera this is the AT dial. On Av auto the camera clicks it for you (the green stop is its pick); tap any speed and it's yours, Tv-style — set the speed, then give it the f-stop the meter asks for. A flashing f-number means your combo misses correct exposure. The amber ″ range is marked orange on the real dial.

Concept: the exposure triangle

a slide rule, independent of the simulator

Exposure is a balance of three things: how long the shutter stays open, how big the aperture hole is, and how sensitive the film is (ISO, also called film speed). The reference here is a correctly exposed scene at 1/1000 · f/5.6 · ISO 400. Push any slider up to add light, down to take it away — then watch what the other two must do to keep the balance.

ISO = film speed: how sensitive the film is to light. On the A-1 you set it once per roll on the ASA dial — analog film cannot change sensitivity from shot to shot, so on film only the shutter and aperture are live controls. Digital cameras can change ISO every frame, which is how ISO became the third dial of the triangle.

Know your Canon A-1

every control, in plain words

The A-1 (1978) was the first SLR with a full electronic brain — nothing works without its 6V battery, and it talks to you through red LED digits in the viewfinder, just like the readout above the simulator. The photos below are the nomenclature pages from your actual manual — find each control by its printed label.

The lens FD 50mm f/1.8

1

Focusing ring

The big front ring. Turn it while watching the circle in the middle of the viewfinder — when the split image lines up, you're focused. The barrel shows the distance in metres and feet, same as the focus slider up top.

2

Aperture ring

Clicks from f/22 to f/1.8 — the exact stops in the simulator. The green A position hands aperture control to the camera body; you need it for Av, Tv and Program modes. Take it off A and you're choosing the f-stop by hand (the finder shows M to warn you).

3

Depth-of-field scale

Pairs of little lines between the two rings, one pair per f-stop. Whatever distances fall between the "16" lines are sharp at f/16. It's this whole simulator, engraved in aluminium.

4

FD mount & release

Line up the red dot on the lens with the dot on the body, seat it, and twist until it locks. Press the release button beside the mount to take it off. Don't force anything — FD mounts are gentle.

Setting exposure front & top, right hand

5

Stop-down / DOF preview lever

Beside the lens mount. The viewfinder always shows the scene at f/1.8, wide open and bright — push this lever and the lens closes down to your real f-stop, so the finder shows the actual depth of field — blur and all — before you commit a frame of film.

6

Self-timer lamp

The red lamp on the front blinks during the 2 or 10-second countdown, so everyone knows when to smile — the dog will ignore it.

7

AT dial

The little thumbwheel at the front, under its flip-up guard. It's the A-1's one input wheel: in Tv it dials shutter speeds, in Av it dials f-numbers. In Tv mode it also has a P position — full Program, camera picks both.

9

AE mode selector

The small switch around the AT dial window. Tv = you pick the shutter speed, camera picks aperture (moving dogs → 1/500). Av = you pick the aperture, camera picks shutter (blurry-background portraits → f/1.8). Lens must be on A for either.

8

Shutter button & main switch

Half-press to wake the meter and light the LEDs, full press to fire. The collar around it is the main switch: A = on, L = locked so it can't fire in your bag. The lever behind it also sets the self-timer (2s / 10s).

11

Exposure compensation + ISO dial

Stacked under the rewind crank. Lift and turn the ISO ring to match your film box — 400 for everyday, 100 for bright sun. The compensation dial (¼× to 4×) deliberately over- or under-exposes, e.g. +1 for a backlit dog so it doesn't become a silhouette.

Film handling top, back & bottom

10

Film advance lever & frame counter

One full stroke with your thumb after every shot — it winds the film one frame and cocks the shutter. The little counter window shows how many of your 24 or 36 frames you've burned.

12

Rewind crank

Fold out the crank and wind clockwise to pull the film back into its canister when the roll is done — but only after pressing the release button (14). Pulling the whole crank straight up pops the back open; never do that mid-roll in daylight.

14

Rewind release button

The small button on the bottom plate. Press it first to let the film run backwards, then crank. If rewinding feels like a fight, this button is what you forgot.

15

Battery & check button

One 6V battery (4LR44) behind the little front door. The A-1's shutter is fully electronic: dead battery = dead camera, no backup speed. Press the battery-check button and the camera beeps fast if it's healthy. Carry a spare.

In the viewfinder 13 & friends

13

Hot shoe

The metal bracket on the prism hump. Slide a flash on and its contacts sync automatically at 1/60 or slower. With a Canon Speedlite A-series flash, the finder confirms when it's charged.

✛

Split-image focusing aid

The circle dead-centre on the screen (drawn on the simulator too). Out of focus, a vertical edge appears broken in half across the middle line; turn the focusing ring until the halves join. The shimmering ring around it (microprism) calms down when focus is right.

88

Red LED readout

Seven-segment digits along the bottom of the finder show shutter speed and f-number — exactly like the readout under the simulator. Whole seconds carry a ″ mark beside the number (4″ = 4 seconds; a plain 4 = 1/4 sec). When the meter runs out of shutter, the speed flashes at its limit: a blinking 1000 means too bright for your f-stop, a blinking 30s means too dark. M means the lens is off the green A — you're setting aperture by hand and the camera is only advising.

…

The small stuff

A lever by the eyepiece closes a blind for self-timer shots (stray light through the eyepiece fools the meter). The exposure memory switch locks a reading while you recompose. There's even a multiple-exposure lever for double exposures. Fun later; ignorable now.

How to develop your film

You can develop your own roll at the kitchen sink with the CineStill JOBO Mono kit. Its Df96 monobath is one bottle that develops and fixes in a single step. The only tricky part is getting the film onto the reel, which has to happen in total darkness inside the dark bag. After that, everything happens with the lights on. Constant: smooth inversions the whole time, changing direction. Intermittent: 30 s constant, then 10 s every minute. Minimal: 10 s gentle, then 5 s every minute. Never let the tank sit still for more than a minute. Temperature tolerance is ±2°F (1°C). Times are minimums: going longer finishes fixing and does not change development.

The dark bag: opening the film and getting it on the reel

This is the one part you do by feel. Film is ruined the moment light touches it, so it all happens inside the zipped bag with your hands through the sleeves. It feels weird the first time. Practise with a junk roll in daylight until you can do it with your eyes shut, and the real thing will be easy.

1

Pack the bag, lights on

Put in: the film, a bottle opener, scissors, the reel, and the tank with its lid. Zip both zips shut. Put your arms in through the elastic sleeves all the way to the elbows. Now feel around and find each thing so you know where it is. Nothing comes out until the lid is on the tank.

2

Pop the cassette open

Feel for the end of the cassette that's flat. The other end has a little knob sticking out; leave that one. Hook the bottle opener under the flat cap's lip and lever it up, like a bottle top. It takes a bit of force. Slide the spool of film out. It's a tight roll on a plastic core. Hold it by the edges, not the flat faces, so you don't leave fingerprints on your photos.

3

Cut the leader, feed the reel

The start of the film has a skinny curved tongue. Cut it off straight so the end is square. Snip the corners off too, so it doesn't snag. Find the reel's entry: two little notches on the outer edge, with tiny balls just inside. Push the square end in past the balls until you feel it grab, about a hand's width.

4

Walk it on, then lid on

Hold one half of the reel in each hand. Twist them in opposite directions, then back. Each twist pulls a bit more film onto the reel. Keep going until you feel the end. Cut the film off the plastic core. Drop the reel into the tank, and screw the lid on tight. Done. Unzip and take everything out.

If it jams: don't force it. Back the film out a little, make sure it isn't folded, and try again. Sweaty hands make the film stick to the reel, so if the bag gets warm, take a break. And if you lose track of what's what in there, stop, breathe, and feel around. Nothing bad happens while the bag stays zipped.

  1. Warm the Df96

    Stand the bottle in a jug of warm water until it reads 75°F / 24°C. Pick your row in the table and set a timer for that time.

  2. Load the reel in the dark bag

    Follow the illustrated guide above. When the tank lid is on, the film is safe and you can take everything out of the bag. Lights stay on from here.

  3. Pour and start the clock

    Pour Df96 in through the lid's light-trap until the reel is covered, tap the tank on the counter to knock loose bubbles, and start the timer.

  4. Agitate as the table says

    For the 4-minute row: 30 seconds of steady inversions, then 10 seconds every minute. Rap the tank after each cycle. Agitation slows development and speeds fixing, so more of it is safe. Letting the tank sit still is not.

  5. Pour back and check

    Through the funnel, back into the bottle. Crack the lid and look at the film edge: it should be clear, not milky. Milky means unfixed. Pour the Df96 back in for another minute or two. Extra time never hurts.

  6. Wash

    Fill the tank with water within 10°F of the chemistry. Invert 5 times, dump. Refill, invert 10 times, dump. Refill, invert 20 times, dump. One last rinse, ideally distilled water with a couple of drops of wetting agent so it dries spot-free.

  7. Hang to dry

    Clip the strip up somewhere dust-free with a weight on the bottom. Squeegee gently with two wet fingers once, or not at all. Give it an hour or two. Then cut into strips of six and sleeve them.

  8. Reuse the Df96

    Every roll you've run through the bottle adds 30 seconds to the next one, up to 8 minutes. Use it within 2 months of opening and keep the bottle full and tightly capped. When it turns dark amber, it's done. Not sure? Snip a bit of leader and drop it in a cup of Df96 in daylight. Fresh chemistry turns it solid black.

The maths under the hood: thin-lens depth-of-field formulas for a 50mm lens on 35mm film, circle of confusion 0.030mm. Blur in the picture is scaled from the true blur-circle size on the negative. The scene meters at EV 12 (overcast, ISO 100); shutter = N²/2^(EV−compensation), rounded to the A-1's 30s–1/1000 steps, flashing at the limit when light and settings can't be reconciled — and the picture washes out or sinks by the stops the exposure actually misses.

How to load a roll

  1. Open the back. Pull the rewind knob straight up until the back door pops. Do this in the shade if you can.
  2. Drop in the cartridge. Cartridge goes in the left chamber, flat end up, film mouth facing the gate. Push the rewind knob back down so its fork seats in the spool.
  3. Pull the leader across. Draw the film over the gate and lay it on the sprocket wheel. Tuck the leader tip into a slot on the take-up spool.
  4. Check the teeth. Wind the advance lever once, slowly. The sprocket teeth must sit in the perforations on both edges and the film should lie flat.
  5. Close the back and press it until it clicks. Fire the shutter and wind on, twice, to get past the exposed leader.
  6. Watch the rewind knob. As you wind, the rewind knob should turn. If it doesn't, the film isn't caught. Open the back and repeat step 3.
  7. Set the ASA dial to the ISO printed on the box. This is the film speed control in the simulator. It stays put for the whole roll.
  8. Frame counter reads 1. You're loaded. When the roll ends the lever will lock. Press the rewind button underneath and crank the rewind knob until it goes slack.
